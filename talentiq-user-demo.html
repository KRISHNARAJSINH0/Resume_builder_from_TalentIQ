What's happening behind the scenes
What Django does

When I click Create Account, React sends a POST /api/auth/register/ to Django. Django hashes my password with PBKDF2, creates a User row in PostgreSQL with role = 'seeker', and returns a JWT access token + refresh token. React stores this token in useContext — now every request I make automatically includes it in the Authorization header.

Why role matters

That role='seeker' field is a gate for everything. When I try to visit /recruiter, Django's IsRecruiter permission class blocks me. When your recruiter mate tries to visit /gap or /interview, they're blocked too. One field, all access control.

How this connects to the recruiter side

The moment I register, I appear in your Talent Pool with a blank profile. As I complete my resume and get a readiness score, your view of me updates in real time. You can see I'm a seeker from Ahmedabad, Sem 4 CS student — even before I apply to any job.

What's happening behind the scenes
The parsing pipeline

PyPDF2 extracts raw text from the PDF. spaCy's NER model finds PERSON (my name), ORG (company names), DATE (graduation year, experience period). A custom skill extractor scans the text against a 500+ skill taxonomy stored in PostgreSQL — if "React.js" appears in my resume AND in the taxonomy, it's flagged as a confirmed skill. Final structured profile saved to the ResumeProfile model with a OneToOne relationship to my User.

Why this is the foundation of everything

Every single other feature reads from this parsed profile. Gap analysis uses my skills. Salary prediction uses my experience + education + skills. Interview simulator picks questions for my target role. Readiness score uses my skill coverage. If the parser gets it wrong, everything downstream is wrong — which is why users can edit any field the parser got incorrect.

How this connects to the recruiter side

Your recruiter dashboard's Talent Pool now shows my profile with parsed skills. When a recruiter searches for "Django candidates in Ahmedabad", I appear. My resume is indexed in PostgreSQL with GIN indexing on the skills array — search is fast even with thousands of candidates.

What's happening behind the scenes
The Pandas gap computation

Django loads all 50+ job listings for "Backend Django Developer" from PostgreSQL. Pandas flattens all the required_skills arrays into one big list and runs value_counts() — counting how often each skill appears across listings. Then a simple Python set subtraction: required_skills_set minus my parsed_skills_set = gap. Each gap skill gets a priority rank based on its frequency count. The ranked DataFrame is serialised as JSON and sent to React.

Why ranking by frequency matters

Not all missing skills are equal. Docker in 89% of listings is critical — learn this first. Celery in 31% is nice-to-have. Without ranking, I'd waste time learning obscure skills while missing the obvious ones. This ranked, data-driven priority list is what no other tool gives you.

How this connects to the recruiter side

The gap data I generate becomes the recruiter's "Skill Shortage Heatmap". If 80% of candidates are missing Docker, your dashboard shows "Docker — Critical Shortage in Backend Pool". This tells you as a recruiter: either lower the bar on Docker, or offer Docker training to hired candidates. My individual gap feeds your market intelligence.

What's happening behind the scenes
TF-IDF + Cosine Similarity

My skill profile and every job listing's required skills are converted into TF-IDF vectors — mathematical representations where each skill is a dimension. Cosine similarity measures the angle between my vector and each job's vector — 1.0 = perfect match, 0 = zero overlap. The top 10 jobs by cosine score are returned. This is the same technique Netflix uses for content recommendations — I've applied it to skill matching.

Why cosine similarity and not just "count matching skills"

Simple skill count doesn't weight importance. Cosine similarity with TF-IDF weights rare skills higher — if I have "FAISS" (rare skill) and a job requires it, that match scores higher than having "Python" (common skill). Rare skill matches signal genuine fit.

How this connects to the recruiter side

When I click "Apply" on a job listing, that application appears in your recruiter pipeline with my readiness score and shortlist probability attached. You don't see a raw resume — you see a ranked candidate with a data-driven score. That's the recruiter side's value: every applicant already has an AI-computed fit score before you open their profile.

What's happening behind the scenes
The ML model chain

Django's salary/predictor.py loads a Random Forest Regressor .pkl file (trained offline in Jupyter on a Kaggle Indian salary dataset). My profile features are built into a numpy array: [0.5, 7, 1, 0, 1, 2]. The model's predict() method returns a single salary value. A confidence interval is computed from the model's tree variance. The "salary boosters" are computed by running the model again with each gap skill added — the salary delta is the booster value.

Why this matters practically

I now know my range is ₹4.2L–₹6.8L. When a recruiter offers me ₹3.5L, I know that's below market. When they offer ₹6L, I know that's fair. I also know that adding Docker to my skillset would push my range up by ₹0.8L — making the gap analysis even more actionable: learn Docker, earn more, immediately.

How this connects to the recruiter side

Your Salary Benchmarks on the recruiter dashboard aggregate all candidate predictions to show: "Backend Django candidates in Ahmedabad expect ₹4.2L–₹6.8L on average." This tells you exactly what the going rate is — so your JD's salary range is competitive, not outdated.

What's happening behind the scenes
The weighted formula

readiness = (0.40 × skill_coverage) + (0.20 × exp_match) + (0.15 × edu_score) + (0.15 × project_score) + (0.10 × interview_score). Each component is normalised 0–1 then multiplied by 100. Django's readiness/calculator.py pulls all component values from their respective models, applies the formula, and saves a new ReadinessScore row with a timestamp. Every time it's computed (on resume update, after interview, after gap refresh), a new row is saved — that's how the trend chart works.

Why one number is powerful

Instead of juggling 5 different metrics, I have one goal: push my score above 70. The breakdown tells me exactly where to focus — my Experience (45%) is dragging the score down. But since I can't magically get more experience, I focus on Skill Coverage (62% → target 85% by adding Docker, AWS, PostgreSQL) which will push the overall score to ~78. One number, clear action.

How this connects to the recruiter side

This score is the primary ranking signal on your candidate list. When you search for "Backend Django candidates", they're sorted by Readiness Score by default. My 67 ranks me above candidates at 50 but below those at 80. You can also filter: "Show only candidates with score > 70" — instantly shortlisting the ready ones without reading a single resume.

What's happening behind the scenes
The 4-signal scoring pipeline

When I submit an answer, Django's interview/scorer.py runs 4 analyses in parallel: (1) VADER sentiment analysis gives compound polarity (-1 to +1) → maps to confidence level. (2) spaCy keyword matching checks overlap between my answer and expected keywords stored in the Question DB row. (3) Flesch Reading Ease score measures how clearly I wrote. (4) Word count vs ideal 50–200 word range. These 4 normalised values feed into a Random Forest classifier (trained on labelled interview answers) → outputs Poor/Average/Good/Excellent. Also stores scores in InterviewAnswer model for session history.

Why instant feedback changes everything

In a real interview, you never know why you failed. Was it your answer content? Your confidence? Your clarity? This simulator tells you exactly — "Your keyword coverage was 6/8, your confidence was high, but your answer structure was weak." You know what to fix before the real interview.

How this connects to the recruiter side

After completing the interview simulator, my Readiness Score updates — the interview_component (10% weight) gets filled in. Your talent pool ranking updates in real time. Also, aggregate interview data from all candidates shows you: "Candidates for Backend Django roles struggle most with System Design questions" — helping you know where to probe in real interviews.

What's happening behind the scenes
Weekly scraping → SkillTrend table → Regression forecast

Every week, Django's manage.py update_trends management command runs the scraper and counts how often each skill appears across all job listings. That count is stored as one row in the SkillTrend table (skill_name, week_date, frequency_count). After 8+ weeks of data, Polynomial Regression is fit on each skill's time series. The fitted curve is extrapolated 12–24 weeks forward to get the forecast. This is the same approach used by financial analysts for stock trend prediction.

Why this is more valuable than the gap analysis

Gap analysis tells me what I need right now. Trends tell me what I need in 6 months. If LangChain is going up 67% by December, I should start learning it now — even if current job listings don't heavily require it yet. I'm not just fixing today's gap, I'm ahead of tomorrow's gap.

How this connects to the recruiter side

Your Hiring Forecast dashboard uses this exact same data — but from a recruiter's perspective. "LangChain will be in 67% more listings by December — start hiring for it now or your JDs will look outdated." The same ML model that advises me to learn a skill advises you to hire for it. User and recruiter data are two sides of one market intelligence system.
